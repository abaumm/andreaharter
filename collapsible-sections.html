Case study

Collapsible Sections: How One Simple Feature Request Became a Full System

Senior UX Designer Business software companies use internally to manage complex case files Live in production. Accessibility testing and real usage numbers aren't finished yet.

The challenge

Add a small arrow icon. Let people click it to close a section. Remember which sections they closed. Done.

That's what the request sounded like: let people close, or "collapse," parts of a form they weren't using right then. Think of a "case" as one record, like a single customer file. Each case's form could have a dozen sections in it: intake details, the people involved, financial fields, a compliance checklist, tables of related records, and internal notes only some people could see. Every section stayed open all the time. Finding the one you needed meant scrolling past ten you didn't.

That request kicked off the project. The project didn't stay that small for long.

The hidden complexity

Once you let people hide content, you've also let them hide problems.

A closed section might be hiding a required field someone forgot to fill in, one that's blocking them from saving the form. Or it might be flagging that some of its content is visible to an outside party on the case. Those are two completely different problems. But if both show up as the same small warning icon on a closed section, a person has no way to tell which one they're looking at until they open it back up.

Remembering which sections someone closed is convenient, until that memory follows them into the wrong case. Open a different case and find sections already closed for no reason you can explain, and you might miss something important inside one of them.

A printed copy of a case is a legal document. It can't be missing sections just because someone had them closed on their screen.

None of that gets fixed by an arrow icon. Fixing it meant building collapsing and expanding as a real system with rules.

Six things that had to work together

Hand this out as nine separate tasks to nine different people, and the pieces end up contradicting each other. I mapped it as one system, with six pieces that all had to agree:

Whether a section starts open or closed by default. What happens when someone clicks to open or close it. How a form error shows up inside a section that's closed. Remembering what someone closed, and knowing exactly when to forget it. How sections nested inside other sections, or tables embedded inside a section, follow the same rules. And printing, which ignores all of that and just shows everything, open or not.

Picture a sample form: an overview section, a section listing everyone involved with a table of related records inside it, a compliance checklist section with several required fields, and a notes section only some people can see. All four had to follow the same six rules, even though they're completely different kinds of content.

The system also had to work for people who move through a form using a keyboard rather than a mouse. If a section is closed, nothing inside it should be reachable by pressing Tab to move through the form. A section's title should only stop your cursor on Tab if clicking it actually does something, not just because it's sitting there. Small rule. Easy to skip if nobody writes it down before the engineers start building.

Three different warnings, and why one icon can't cover all of them

Awareness means "there's something worth noticing in here," nothing more. It doesn't mean the person did anything wrong. Blocking means the opposite: the form can't be saved until something changes, and it needs to look more urgent than awareness at a glance. Party Access is neither of those. It just means an outside party can see part of this section, which has nothing to do with errors at all.

Early versions used one icon for all three. I pushed hard to split them apart, because a person looking at that icon needs to know whether it means "worth a glance," "fix this before you can save," or "someone outside the company can see this," and one shape can't tell them which.

We ended up with a shared icon that changes in two ways at once: an outline for awareness, a solid fill for blocking, plus a color change on top of that. That way the two don't depend on color alone to look different. Party Access kept its own separate icon entirely. We also considered a fourth icon, for fields that show up later because of some change elsewhere in the process, and left it out of this release. The rules for when it would appear and disappear weren't settled, and shipping it half-finished would have confused people more than not having it at all.

Why errors don't show up until after you click Save

A form covered in red errors before you've typed a single word doesn't feel careful. It feels broken.

The red warning icon on a section stays off on a brand-new case, and only turns on after someone tries to save with required fields still empty. The little marker next to a required field, like an asterisk, never turns red. An empty field gets a short red message underneath it, something like "Required."

In this system, an error only shows up because of something a person did, not just because the page loaded. That's why a brand-new case starts out looking like a plain blank form. Someone who saves with real gaps left gets a warning that's impossible to miss, plus a way to jump straight to whatever section is hiding the problem.

Why the memory of closed sections only applies to one case

Remembering which sections someone closed is genuinely useful, as long as they're back in the same case. It turns into a real problem the moment that memory leaks into a different case. Sections start out closed for no reason anyone can explain, and a person can miss content in a completely different case because it inherited a memory that belonged to the last one.

We tied that memory to one specific case and one specific person's browser. It survives closing and reopening browser tabs, closing and reopening the whole browser, refreshing the page, and logging out and back in. It never carries over from one case to another. That boundary also raised a set of testing questions: what happens if someone clears their browser's saved data, or two people share a computer and log in as different users, or someone opens the form in a private browsing window, like Incognito mode. You only catch questions like those by asking what the memory is actually for, not just whether it technically works.

Keeping the design file and the real plan from disagreeing

Partway through the project, our design file (the document showing what everything should look like) fell behind. Decisions kept changing in our project-tracking tool faster than anyone could update the pictures to match. Two documents that used to say the same thing stopped agreeing with each other.

I keep a running personal record of every decision as it's made, and use AI-assisted tools to turn that into an updated task entry fast, often right after the decision happens. That speed is what actually kept the mismatch from spreading: any engineer, or any AI tool, reading the task entry got the current, correct answer, even while the picture-based document was still out of date. I also wrote down exactly what not to assume from the old document. That kept anyone, human or AI, from pulling an outdated idea back out of it and treating it as current.

Without that, an engineer building from the old document would have shipped the wrong icon and called it finished.

The team, engineers and testers together, checked a specific list of situations this model made visible: the same person looking at different cases, what happens on refresh and logout, cleared browser data and private browsing, sections nested inside each other with long titles, the moment right after someone's first save attempt, and how the whole thing looks printed.

What actually happened, and what's still unknown

Collapsible Sections is live in production now. The team built one complete, consistent set of rules covering how sections get set up, how they behave when clicked, how errors show up inside them, what gets remembered, and how everything prints. What each icon means, when warnings appear, and what's in or out of this version are all written down clearly enough for engineers and testers to build and check against.

We expect people to scan long forms faster, feel less overwhelmed by them, miss fewer required fields hidden inside closed sections, and get cleaner printouts. Screen readers (the software blind users rely on to have a page read aloud) and keyboard use both shaped these decisions from the very start, for sections open and closed alike.

Add a small arrow icon. Remember which sections someone closed. Done. That's still what it looks like from the outside. Look closer, and there are six behaviors that all had to agree with each other, three warning icons that aren't allowed to blur into one, and a rule about exactly when error messages appear that took a full team discussion to get right.
